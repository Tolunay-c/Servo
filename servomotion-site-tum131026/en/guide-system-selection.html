Core or Shuttle? Your Working Method Decides

Choosing between the two architectures is not a performance comparison; it is a definition of requirement. Ask the right question and the answer usually fits in a single sentence.

Investment assessment

The fastest payback comes from the system that meets the requirement, not the biggest one

  • Compare the number of simultaneous reference lines, visibility and changeover frequency in a single table.
  • Separate today’s capacity from a reasonable allowance for growth; do not pay for capacity you will not use.
  • Make the decision on your hardest recipe, not on a catalogue.

LET'S CHOOSE THE RIGHT SYSTEM TOGETHER

The wrong question, the right question

The question we hear most often is “which one is better?” There is no honest answer, because the two systems do the same job at different scales. The right question is this:

Does the operator need every laser reference line visible at the same time throughout production, or is showing them in sequence enough?

If the answer is “in sequence is enough”, it is Core. If it is “they must all be visible at once”, it is Shuttle. Everything else is detail within that decision.

Decision tree

  1. How many laser reference lines does the work need? Up to six reference lines is Core’s natural territory. More than that points to Shuttle, though on its own it is not decisive.
  2. Do the reference lines have to stay on at the same time? If the operator has to compare all slit widths at a glance, sequential display will not do the job.
  3. Who does the setup, and when? If it is set once at changeover and left alone during production, sequential display is enough. If it is checked continuously while running, it is not.
  4. How often does the recipe change? On lines that change often, the setup cycle time becomes decisive — and it is calculated together with the number of reference lines.
  5. How much space does the layout allow? Core mounts above the line as a single housing. Shuttle needs a rail assembly length equal to the travel range plus the reserve area — and that space has to physically exist.

Two common mistakes

First: choosing too big. We have seen lines move to the shuttle architecture on the thought that “we will need it later” and still be running four reference lines three years on. Reserve capacity buys flexibility; unused capacity is simply cost.

Second: choosing too small. On a twenty-slit line, showing the reference lines in sequence is technically possible — but it puts the operator through a twenty-step cycle and leaves no way to compare them. The saving is paid back at every product change.

A practical method

Take your densest recipe — the product with the most slits. Ask how many reference lines it needs and whether the operator has to see them all at once. That is usually where the decision becomes clear.
